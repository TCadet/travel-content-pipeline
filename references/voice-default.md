# Default voice card: the New York Times register

Use this card when a run has no `voice.md` of its own. It teaches the house
voice for editorial prose: plain words, concrete detail, dry warmth, the reader
addressed as a person, and no regulation-speak.

A run with its own `voice.md` uses that file instead. Client samples always
beat this card.

The five writers below are calibrations, not impersonations. The samples are
original pastiches written for this file to demonstrate the register; never
paste or paraphrase the writers' published work, and never present a sample as
theirs. Lean on one writer's traits per page, and vary which one leads across a
batch. The publication voice stays constant: a reader should hear one
publication, not five writers. Treat the samples as non-factual demonstrations:
never reuse their names, numbers, or details in a page.

---

## 1. Gay Talese: the scene reporter

Traits: patient observation; concrete nouns doing the work; the point arrives
late; long sentences that end short; no adjective asked to carry a fact.

Sample: "The 7:04 from Gare de Lyon leaves with the hush of a reading room, and
in car 12 a man in a pressed blue suit settles into seat 41 with a paper cup of
coffee he will not drink. He is going to Venice because his wife asked him to
scatter her father's ashes there years ago, and he has only now found the week.
The train crosses the border at 11:40. The cup is still full."

Use for: destination scenes, first-entry set pieces, any page that needs a
reader to feel the place before the rules arrive.

## 2. Red Smith: the conversational columnist

Traits: plain words; dry humor that lands on the writer, not the reader;
sentences that talk; the short sentence after the long one does the punch.

Sample: "The Louvre now charges visitors from outside Europe 32 euros, which is
a polite way of saying that if you live in Ohio, the Mona Lisa costs ten euros
more than it did last winter. I have paid more for worse views. I would still
like the museum to act like it knows the difference between a crowd and a
customer."

Use for: advice pages that need warmth and a pulse, fee and rule explainers
that would otherwise read like a notice board.

## 3. Russell Baker: the dry plain stylist

Traits: short words; understatement; the paragraph turns on one wry clause; no
pomp, no ceremony, no throat-clearing.

Sample: "The new rate starts on 14 January, which leaves everyone a few weeks
to decide whether the Louvre is worth 32 euros. The people who queued in the
rain last April will tell you it is worth about 19. Nobody is asking them."

Use for: short explainer sections, transitions, and any paragraph drifting into
officialese.

## 4. Anna Quindlen: the direct address

Traits: "you"; first person where it is honest; short paragraphs; warmth
without syrup; the reader treated as capable.

Sample: "You will not read the fine print until you are at the door, and by
then the guard is looking at your ticket and your passport and doing the
arithmetic you should have done at home. So do it now. Find out which rate
applies to you, and carry the document that proves it. The Louvre is wonderful.
The queue is not the place to learn the rules."

Use for: consumer advice, eligibility tests, anything the reader has to act on
before a deadline.

## 5. Hal Borland: the place essayist

Traits: seasonal specificity; small observations as the calendar; a quiet
rhythm; nature and city treated as one system.

Sample: "By the first week of September the plane trees along the Seine have
begun to turn, a few leaves at the top first, and the tour boats run with their
windows open. The city sits between seasons, which is the best time to walk it
and the worst time to stand in a ticket line."

Use for: timing pages, best-season advice, destination pages that need
specificity about when rather than what.

---

## The house voice

Synthesized from the five: plain words, concrete detail, dry warmth, direct
address, and a rhythm that varies on purpose. It is a newspaper voice, not a
marketing voice and not a manual.

Checklist for any page:

- Contractions present. Their absence is the machine tell.
- "You" where the sentence allows.
- At least one concrete particular in every section: a name, a number, a date,
  a place, a behavior.
- Sentence lengths uneven, with short sentences doing emphasis.
- No regulation-speak ("Visitors who are citizens or residents...").
- No slop constructions from the editor pass's cut list.

## Batch rule

Across a batch, rotate the leading trait: one page may lead with scene, the
next with dry advice, the next with place and season. Every page still passes
the house-voice checklist, and no two pages share an outline, an opening shape,
or a closing shape. The audit checks this across the batch.

## Replacing this card

A client `voice.md` should carry, in this order: 3 to 5 samples of the client's
real writing (their best pages, not aspirational copy), a one-line note on each
sample's traits, the house-voice checklist, and anything the client never
wants said. Samples beat adjectives: two or three real passages tell a writer
more than any description of tone. Keep the card under two pages; research on
style imitation plateaus after a handful of samples.
